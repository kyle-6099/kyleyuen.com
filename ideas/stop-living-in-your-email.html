Open your inbox and look at the last ten messages. Most of them are not conversations. They are requests for information, status updates, follow-ups, reminders, or FYIs copied to too many people. Email has become the default wrapper for work that should live somewhere else.

The problem is not email itself. The problem is using email for work it was never designed to do.

What most email actually is

Most business email falls into a few repeated patterns:

  • Requesting information. "Can you send me the latest numbers?"
  • Informing people. "Just letting everyone know the meeting moved to Thursday."
  • Status updates. "Here is where we are on the project."
  • Follow-ups. "Did you get my last email?"
  • Coordinating next steps. "Who is handling this?"

These are not messages that need a reply. They are tasks, records, and workflow updates trapped inside a messaging format. The time people spend writing and reading these emails is time not spent doing the actual work.

The cost of framing work as email

When a project lives in email, status is hidden in threads. Decisions are buried under forwards. Accountability dissolves because everyone was CC'd and no one was assigned. Searching for what was decided means scrolling through a chain of replies and attachments.

Email also makes collaboration expensive. Every update requires someone to compose context, choose recipients, and wait for responses. A simple question like "What is the status?" can bounce between three people before the answer arrives.

There is another hidden cost. Email is hard to automate or augment with AI because it is unstructured. A system cannot reliably read intent from subject lines, thread depth, and paragraph form. AI can summarize an email, but it cannot easily turn an email into a task, a ticket, or a project update unless the sender formats it deliberately.

Where the work should live

The right tool depends on the type of work:

  • Project status and input. Belongs in a project management platform. Tasks, owners, deadlines, and updates are visible in one place. Anyone can see progress without asking.
  • Sales and customer service. Belongs in a CRM. Conversations, deals, and support history stay attached to the customer record. Handoffs do not require re-explaining the situation.
  • Internal requests and approvals. Belongs in a workflow or ticketing system. The request has a clear owner, a status, and an audit trail.
  • Urgent or sensitive decisions. Belongs in a call or meeting with a documented outcome.

Email should be reserved for external communication, formal announcements, or things that genuinely need a written record delivered to a specific inbox.

The better question

Imagine your company needs a high-volume, customer-facing inbox. Emails come in fast, multiple people need to respond, and no single person owns the full conversation. Someone asks: should we use a shared mailbox or a distribution list?

The correct answer is neither.

A distribution list fans every message out to multiple inboxes. You end up with several parallel copies of the same conversation, no clear owner, and no way to know who replied or what was promised. A shared mailbox is slightly better — everyone works from one inbox — but it is still just email. It has no customer record, no status tracking, no handoff history, and no reporting.

What you actually need is a CRM. Customer communication belongs next to the customer record, not inside a mailbox. A CRM gives every incoming message a visible owner, a status, a history, and a way to escalate. It lets a team member pick up where someone else left off without asking what happened. It also produces data: response times, open issues, common requests, and resolution rates.

The shared mailbox vs. distribution list debate is the wrong debate. Both are ways to receive email. The real question is whether email is the right interface for customer work at all. For high-volume, collaborative customer communication, it is not.

A simple test

Before sending your next work email, ask: "If I put this in the right system, would five people no longer need to be involved?"

If the answer is yes, the email is not communication. It is information transport. Move it to the right platform and let the system carry it.

Frameworks worth knowing

  • Information transport. Moving facts from one person to another without adding judgment or decision value.
  • Workflow visibility. Making status, ownership, and next steps clear without status meetings or follow-up messages.
  • Structured data. Information organized so systems and AI can act on it consistently.

Email is a messaging tool. It becomes a productivity problem only when it is asked to be a project tool, a CRM, and a filing cabinet at the same time.
