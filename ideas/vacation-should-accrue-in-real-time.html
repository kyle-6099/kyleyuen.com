Most traditional employers treat the statutory minimum as the whole policy. In British Columbia, the Employment Standards Act sets two entitlements: vacation time and vacation pay. But treating the legal floor as the operating design ignores the math underneath, and that math gets expensive.

What the law actually says

In BC, employees earn two entitlements. After 12 consecutive months of employment, a regular employee is entitled to at least 2 weeks off; after 5 consecutive years, at least 3 weeks. Vacation pay is 4% of total wages for the first five years, then 6% after that. (BC ESA, Part 7, ss. 57–58, bclaws.gov.bc.ca.)

The ESA also says the employer must ensure the employee takes the annual vacation within 12 months after completing the year of employment that earned it. (BC ESA, s. 57(2).)

Where the statutory minimum creates cost and timing problems

For a salaried employee who gets a raise, the vacation pay earned at the old wage rate may not match two weeks at the new salary. The ESA does not require the employer to top it up; some employers choose to so the employee continues to receive their current salary during vacation, while others pay out only what was accrued. Either way, the company has to decide how to handle the gap.

The same issue applies to wage employees. A wage worker who moves to a higher hourly rate will have vacation pay accrued at the old rate. If they take time off at the new rate, the accrued pool may not cover the full day.

The 12-month take-it window also creates pressure. If the balance is allowed to compound for multiple years, the employer can end up paying out time that was earned at older, lower wage rates at the employee’s current salary. That is not a legal issue, but it is a cost issue.

Why some employers ignore the math

Some employers prefer flexibility over strict timing. They let employees carry balances forward, even though it costs more later. Others front-load the full vacation bank at the start of the year or on the anniversary, which creates the same inflation risk and makes coverage harder to plan.

The cleanest answer is not to fight the timing rule. It is to stop pretending vacation is earned once a year and start accruing it each payroll.

How the math changes by employment type

For a salaried employee, vacation pay is usually embedded in regular payroll. The accrual is still happening in the background, even if it is not visible on the payslip. The real question is whether the balance shown to the employee matches the actual dollars available.

For part-time or hourly employees, the math is more visible. A part-time worker paid $500 in a pay period accrues vacation pay at 4%, or $20, each pay run. If they take a vacation day later, the pay they receive for that day should reflect their average earnings, not a flat guess.

The case for real-time accrual

BC law only grants the days after one full year. But think about it this way: if you would let an employee take their earned ten days in the last ten working days of their first year, then they have already earned that time. It just happens to become visible on the anniversary. If the time is earned continuously, there is no logical reason to block access until day 365.

Accruing days and dollars on every payroll fixes the inflation problem too. Each pay period sets aside the right percentage of current wages. The balance stays current with the employee’s actual earnings. There is no big catch-up top-up later.

What a clean system looks like

In a clean setup, the payroll engine accrues vacation pay as a percentage of wages every cycle. The time-off module accrues days at the same pace. When an employee requests a day off, the system checks both balances: do they have enough accrued time, and is there enough accrued pay to cover the day at their average earnings rate?

If the answer is yes, the leave is approved and payroll pays it automatically. If the answer is no, the request is either blocked or flagged. There is no spreadsheet on the side, no manager guessing at balances, and no finance team reconciling liabilities after the fact.

The bottom line

Vacation is not a reward that drops after twelve months. It is compensation that is earned continuously. The law already separates days from dollars. A well-run system should accrue both in real time, let employees access leave as they earn it, and let the platform handle the math. That reduces cost inflation for the company and gives employees a benefit that actually matches how they work.
